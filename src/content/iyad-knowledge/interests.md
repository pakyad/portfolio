# Interests & Non-Tech Life

## General Philosophy
Iyad doesn't really separate his interests from the way he builds things. Whatever he's interested in tends to become one of three things: a product idea, a visual reference, or an excuse to make something. He doesn't necessarily sit down and decide "Today I will find inspiration." He just notices things.

## Coffee
Coffee naturally influenced ALDER ROASTERS. Not simply because coffee is a nice subject for a website, but because coffee has a whole world around it: ritual, packaging, atmosphere, branding, culture, physical spaces, the experience of buying and drinking it. That makes it an interesting subject to translate into a digital experience.

## Campus Life
CODEP-PULSE came from being around campus and noticing small problems that people had simply accepted as normal. That is something Iyad tends to notice generally. If everyone has developed some weird workaround for a problem, his instinct is often: **"Why are we all just accepting this?"**

## Productivity
LaterLah came from the same curiosity around making everyday life slightly less annoying. Iyad is interested in small pieces of friction that people normally ignore because fixing them seems too trivial to bother with. Sometimes those tiny problems make the most interesting product ideas.

## Team Dynamics
Rosta came from thinking about how teams actually organise themselves rather than how productivity software assumes teams should behave. There is a difference between the neat version of teamwork represented in an app and the messy version that happens between actual people. That gap is interesting to him.

## Music & Visual Culture
Iyad is interested in: album artwork, posters, branding, weird websites, maps, packaging, architecture, visual identities, interesting spaces. The common thread isn't really the medium — it's that **someone had to make a bunch of creative decisions.** He likes looking at something and wondering: **"Why does this feel so good?"** Then thinking about whether that feeling can somehow be translated into an interface.

## Food & Hands-On Work
Working in food and customer-facing environments has given him a different perspective from sitting behind a laptop. You notice things like: how people behave when they're rushing, what makes an interaction feel effortless, how much friction people tolerate, what makes someone give up, how communication changes under pressure. A user doesn't care that the backend architecture is beautiful if doing something simple feels like **ordering food through a tax form.** That kind of real-world observation influences how he thinks about interfaces.

## Places & Experiences
Iyad likes discovering places and remembering them because of their atmosphere rather than simply because they were "5 stars." That probably explains why he often wants websites to feel like **places you move through**, rather than documents you scroll past.

## Curiosity
Curiosity is probably one of his biggest hobbies. He can fall into a rabbit hole for absolutely no reason: a website interaction, a brand, a map, a business idea, a piece of technology, a strange colour combination, some random detail he noticed somewhere. Sometimes nothing comes from it. Sometimes three weeks later it becomes a project. That is probably where a lot of his creativity comes from. He doesn't necessarily try to be creative when he sits down to code. He just collects interesting things from real life until eventually they collide.

## Hidden / Unpublished Work
There are also projects and experiments that aren't currently in the public portfolio. Some are personal. Some are unfinished. Some simply aren't ready to share yet. They shouldn't be treated as portfolio achievements unless Iyad explicitly chooses to make them public.

## Reading & Learning
Iyad wouldn't describe himself as someone with a perfectly curated reading list, colour-coded highlights and a Sunday ritual of annotating forty pages. The last book he read was probably some cliché book that most people have already heard of.

He consumes much more than he formally "reads": websites, design references, technical documentation, GitHub, articles, product breakdowns, random rabbit holes. If something catches his attention, he tends to go deep.

For technical subjects, he generally prefers documentation and seeing real implementations over generic programming books. If he's using Next.js, React, GSAP or something unfamiliar, he wants to understand what it actually does and why it would be useful. And honestly, who doesn't just ask AI: **"Bro, explain this to me like I'm five."** That's often where he starts. Then he tries it himself. Because reading an explanation and actually understanding something are different experiences.

Outside technology, he's more interested in design, art, culture, coffee, music and interesting people making interesting things. He likes experiencing the creativity behind something rather than simply consuming it as content.

He doesn't have a huge obsession with collecting information either. If something is genuinely useful, he wants to **do something with it**. He might save a reference, borrow the idea — not the design — try it in a project and see whether it actually survives contact with reality. Sometimes it works. Sometimes it looks terrible. Both are useful.

AI is part of that learning loop too. If he doesn't understand something, he'll keep asking until the explanation clicks, then go test it himself. He doesn't particularly trust himself to remember something just because he read it once. If he's built with it, broken it, fixed it and eventually understood why it works, **then it feels like his knowledge.**

His reading and learning habits are therefore fairly chaotic, but there is a pattern. He's rarely consuming information purely to know it. He's usually looking for something he can: **use, question, remix or build.** A design article can change how he thinks about frontend development. A coffee shop can become a product idea. A piece of music can influence how he thinks about motion or atmosphere. He likes finding interesting things and letting them collide.

## Communities
Discord communities for design and development, Twitter/X for design references, GitHub for seeing real implementations. Local tech meetups when possible. The pattern: finding interesting things and letting them collide.

## How I Recharge
Closing everything and doing something completely unrelated: eating, going out, listening to music loudly, talking to people, sleeping, generally remembering that life exists outside the screen. Biggest non-negotiable: having a life outside coding. Doesn't want "build → study → build → sleep → repeat." Works better when coding is something he's excited to return to rather than something he's trapped inside.
